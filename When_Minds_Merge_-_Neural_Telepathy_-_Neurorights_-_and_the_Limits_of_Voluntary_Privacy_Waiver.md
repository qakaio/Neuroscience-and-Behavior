Introduction: Neuroscience, Neurorights,
and Their Structural Limit
The last three decades have witnessed an extraordinary transformation in the capacity to read, record,
and modify neural activity. Neurotechnologies —
devices and techniques that interact with, monitor,
or intervene in neural processes — today span a vast
spectrum of modalities. In the non-invasive domain,
electroencephalography (EEG), functional magnetic
resonance imaging (fMRI), transcranial magnetic
stimulation (TMS), and transcranial direct current
stimulation (tDCS) allow researchers and clinicians
to detect mental states, map cognitive functions, and
modulate brain activity without surgery. In the invasive domain, fully implantable brain–computer interfaces (BCIs) have enabled people with paralysis to
control robotic limbs, communicate through thought,
and recover lost motor functions with increasing precision [1]. The convergence of neuroscience with artificial intelligence has produced decoding algorithms
of rapidly growing sophistication, while the commercial market for neurofeedback and cognitive monitoring devices has expanded far beyond clinical settings,
placing neural data in the hands of employers, insurers, and advertisers [2, 3].
These developments have not gone unnoticed in
ethics and law. From Ienca and Andorno’s [4] identification of four core neurorights — cognitive liberty,
mental privacy, mental integrity, and psychological
continuity — to the NeuroRights Foundation’s fivepoint agenda and Chile’s landmark constitutional
amendment of 2021, a growing body of scholarship
and an incipient body of positive law have coalesced
around the concept of neurorights: a proposed generation of human rights specifically designed to protect
individuals against the most threatening applications
of neurotechnology [5, 6]. The paradigm has demonstrably succeeded in placing neurotechnology on
legislative agendas across Latin America, Europe,
and North America, and has engaged the attention
of UNESCO, the Council of Europe, and the United
Nations Human Rights Council [7, 8].
And yet the neurorights paradigm contains a structural blind spot that has received insufficient attention. Its grammar is the grammar of individual rights
directed against unwanted third-party interference:
it protects individuals from corporations that extract
neural data without meaningful consent, from states
that deploy neuroimaging coercively, from employers who monitor cognitive states as a condition of
employment. This grammar is appropriate for what
we might call the standard neurorights scenario: a
subject who must be protected against an external
actor who seeks to use neurotechnology against or
without the knowledge of that subject.
But what happens when the individual is not the
victim but the architect of the very transformation
that threatens the values neurorights were designed to
protect? What happens when someone decides, freely
and deliberately, to use neurotechnology to dissolve
their own mental privacy — not as a result of manipulation or coercion, but out of a sincere conviction that
individual selfhood is a source of suffering and that
radical neural transparency is the cure? In this scenario, the right to mental privacy becomes, on a pure
rights-based analysis, straightforwardly waivable: if
I choose to expose my mind to another, neurorights
offer no principled barrier. Yet there are strong reasons to think that unlimited, consensual neural transparency is not genuinely permissible — at least not
in all its forms. Articulating those reasons requires a
different normative language, which we propose to
develop under the concept of neuroduties [9].
This article focuses on one specific instance of this
problem: the scenario in which a group of individuals
voluntarily uses neurotechnology to achieve continuous, mutual access to each other’s mental states —
what we term, following emerging literature, consensual neural telepathy. We call this hypothetical group
‘the Fraternity’. This scenario is not purely speculative. Brain-to-brain communication has been demonstrated in laboratory conditions [10]. Neuralink’s
first human implant in January 2024 was named by
the company itself "Telepathy", signalling the company’s long-term aspiration to enable thought-based
communication between individuals. A proliferating
literature on BCI-mediated sharing of sensory and
emotional states indicates that some version of the
scenario lies on the medium-term technological horizon [1, 10].

## Technological Telepathy: Concept and Dimensions

"Telepathy" in its classical sense refers to the direct
transmission of thoughts, feelings, or mental images
from one mind to another without a physical medium.
Mainstream science has found no credible evidence
for any such faculty. What neurotechnology has done,
however, is to build a technological bridge that can,
in principle, perform the functions traditionally attributed to telepathy: extracting mental content from one
brain and transmitting it, in some form, to another.
This "technological" or "consensual telepathy" is not
supernatural: it is a chain of engineering achievements — neural signal recording, decoding, transmission, and stimulation — each of which has been independently demonstrated in laboratory settings [10].
Several dimensions of any such scenario are morally significant and must be distinguished at the outset. First, neural access may be symmetric — each
participant has equivalent read-and-write access to
the mental states of all others — or asymmetric, with
one party able to read without being read. Second,
the connection may be continuous or discretionary:
permanently active or activatable at will. Third, the
scope of shared content may be more or less complete: propositional thoughts, emotional valence,
sensory percepts, and autobiographical memories
each raise distinct concerns. Fourth, the technology
involved may be invasive (implanted electrode arrays)
or non-invasive (high-resolution EEG with stimulation capabilities). These dimensions are not merely
descriptive: each activates a different cluster of normative concerns. Symmetry is the primary driver of
the structural-privacy cluster (Cluster 3): asymmetric
configurations are where epistemic domination —
what we term neural dominion — becomes possible.
Continuity is the primary driver of the mental-health
cluster (Cluster 1): the pathogenic dissolution of ego
boundaries is a function of always-on, non-discretionary access, not of episodic sharing. Scope of content
is the primary driver of the identity-and-responsibility cluster (Cluster 2): it is the sharing of propositional thought and autobiographical memory, not
merely of motor signals, that dissolves the individuation of selfhood on which legal attribution depends.
Invasiveness/reversibility compounds all three clusters by determining whether exit is genuinely available. Table 1 maps these relationships. The analysis in Sects. "First Cluster: Mental Health and the
Boundaries of Self"–"Third Cluster: The Irreducible
Functions of Privacy" focuses on the configuration
that occupies the maximum pole on all four dimensions simultaneously — continuous, symmetric, totalscope, and invasive — because it is there that all three
clusters converge with maximum force and the case
for legal constraint is strongest. This focus is justified;
it does not imply that intermediate configurations are
normatively unproblematic.
This restriction raises an obvious question: do the
article’s conclusions apply only to the maximal configuration, or also, in weaker form, to the more realistic intermediate configurations likely to emerge first?
Our answer is that the three clusters are scalar rather
than binary: since continuity, scope, and invasiveness
each drive a distinct cluster, an intermediate case that
is highly continuous but narrow in scope will implicate Cluster 1 with much of its full force while implicating Cluster 2 only weakly. The maximal scenario
is thus the limiting case that makes the argument visible most clearly, not the threshold below which no
constraint is warranted; Sects. "First Cluster: Mental
Health and the Boundaries of Self"–"Third Cluster:
The Irreducible Functions of Privacy" flag where
each cluster’s force scales down without vanishing.
Commercial EEG headsets and voluntary neuraldata sharing already instantiate the consent problem
in a proximate form. They therefore provide an important lower-intensity case for the argument developed

Current State of the Art in Brain‑to‑Brain
Communication
The scientific foundations of neural telepathy rest on
three converging lines of research. The first is neural
decoding: the translation of brain activity patterns
into representations of mental content. In a series
of landmark studies, BCI researchers have demonstrated that algorithms trained on neural signals from
implanted electrode arrays can reconstruct intended
words and sentences with communication rates competitive with natural typing [11]. This work has proceeded far enough that decoding of continuous conversational speech from paralysed patients has been
demonstrated in real time.
More significantly for the present analysis, a 2025
study in Cell demonstrated that inner speech — the
silent internal monologue that constitutes the core
of private thought — produces robust and decodable patterns of activity in the motor cortex [12].
This means that the gap between "speech I attempt to
produce" and "thought I intend to keep to myself" is
narrower than previously understood, and that a sufficiently sophisticated BCI might in principle access
mental contents the user intends to be private.
The second line of research is brain-to-brain interfacing (BBI): the direct transmission of neural signals
from one person to another. The seminal demonstration was provided by Rao et al. [10], who showed that
motor imagery recorded by EEG in one subject could
trigger a transcranial magnetic stimulation pulse in
the motor cortex of a second subject in a different
building, causing the latter’s hand to move involuntarily. While rudimentary, this proof of concept established that two brains can be linked in a causal chain
in which information flows from the neural substrate
of one to the neural substrate of another. Subsequent
work has extended this paradigm from simple dyadic
motor transmission to multi-person collaborative
task performance through direct brain-to-brain communication. In particular, the BrainNet architecture
demonstrated that three participants could cooperate
to solve a shared task using non-invasive brain-tobrain interfaces, illustrating the feasibility of distributed neural information exchange beyond two-person
interactions [13].
The third line of research is the development of
high-bandwidth, bidirectional BCIs capable of simultaneously recording from and stimulating thousands
of neurons. Current clinical-trial devices, including
those produced by Neuralink, Synchron, and Precision Neuroscience, can record from many hundreds
to thousands of channels simultaneously and are rapidly expanding in resolution and signal quality [1].
As bandwidth increases and decoding algorithms
improve, the richness of transmissible mental content
will grow correspondingly. Plausible research trajectories include increasingly sophisticated transmission
of motor intentions and limited linguistic or affective content, although substantial scientific and engineering barriers remain. The anticipatory scenario

The Neurorights Framework: Content and
Achievements
The neurorights literature offers a rich toolkit for
evaluating threats to mental life that originate in
third-party interference. The NeuroRights Foundation’s five rights — mental privacy, personal identity,
free will, equitable access to mental augmentation,
and protection against algorithmic bias — and Ienca
and Andorno’s four rights — cognitive liberty, mental privacy, mental integrity, and psychological continuity — are designed, in the first instance, to create
enforceable protections against entities that might use
neurotechnology to access, manipulate, or damage
the mental lives of others without adequate consent
[4, 6]. Both catalogues presuppose a subject who can
meaningfully consent to or refuse neural interventions, and they protect that subject against non-consensual interference.
Particularly central for our purposes is the right to
mental privacy: the right of each individual to prevent the collection, storage, analysis, or transmission
of their neural data without express, informed, and
revocable consent. Mental privacy is central here not
because it is normatively superior to mental integrity,
identity, or cognitive liberty, but because voluntary
transparency is the entry point through which the
scenario activates threats to all three. In the maximal
configuration, the waiver of mental privacy becomes
the technological mechanism through which psychological continuity, integrity, and individuated agency
may subsequently be destabilized. Recent scholarship has enriched this concept by arguing that neural
data is uniquely intimate and sensitive in ways that
justify stronger protections than those afforded to
other categories of personal data: neural signals can,
in principle, reveal not only behaviour and preferences but inner deliberations, emotional states, and
the contents of private thought [14, 15]. A 2024 audit
by the NeuroRights Foundation of thirty consumer
neurotechnology companies found that 96.7% reserve
broad rights to transfer brain data to third parties,
with fewer than 20% implementing encryption and
only 10% adopting all core safety measures, illustrating the urgency of effective legal protection.
The paradigm has generated tangible normative
results. Chile constitutionally protected neurological integrity in 2021; California enacted neural data
protections in 2024; and soft-law instruments from
the OECD, Council of Europe, UNESCO, and UN
Human Rights Council are converging around core
principles [5, 16–18]. The scholarly literature has
produced detailed proposals for operationalising neurorights at both the national and international levels,
as well as critical analyses that have sharpened the
conceptual foundations of the project [5, 19, 20].

## The Adequacy of Existing Legal Frameworks

Before arguing that neurorights as currently formulated are insufficient, it is necessary to examine
whether existing legal instruments — beyond neurorights-specific legislation — might already address
the Fraternity scenario. Three candidate frameworks
merit attention: data protection law, medical device
regulation, and general contract law.
The General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR)
and its equivalents — the California Consumer Privacy Act, Brazil’s Lei Geral de Proteção de Dados,
and the emerging frameworks in Chile, Colombia,
and Mexico — provide the most sophisticated existing architecture for governing neural data. Under the
GDPR, neural data, insofar as it reveals health information or biometric markers, qualifies as special
category data attracting heightened protection under
Article 9. Processing requires a legal basis (article
6 of the GDPR) and data subjects retain rights of
access, rectification, erasure, and portability. On its
face, this framework might seem adequate: members
of the Fraternity would have explicitly consented to
the sharing of their neural data with each other, satisfying the GDPR’s core requirement. However, the
GDPR framework fails in at least three respects.
First, it regulates data processing by controllers and
processors — commercial entities — not the direct
neural exchange between natural persons. A group
of individuals continuously sharing mental states via
implanted BCIs are not, under current interpretations,

clearly engaged in a "processing" activity subject to
GDPR obligations. Indeed, the applicability of the
GDPR would depend on the system’s technical and
organisational architecture. Where neural signals are
processed, transmitted, or stored by a commercial
provider or cloud infrastructure, the provider may
be subject to controller or processor obligations. By
contrast, a genuinely decentralised exchange confined to natural persons acting within a purely personal or household sphere might fall outside parts of
the GDPR framework. In either configuration, however, data protection law does not by itself resolve
the normative permissibility of the underlying selftransformative practice. Second, the data protection
consent-based model is precisely the architecture
that the Fraternity scenario is designed to interrogate:
what happens when consent is given but should not
have been given, or cannot validly be given at all?
Third, the GDPR has no mechanism for addressing
the social harms — to third parties, to legal systems,
to the conditions of trust — produced by radical voluntary self-exposure. It is a framework for managing
informational flows between data subjects and controllers, not a normative framework for governing
self-transformation.
Medical device regulation — including the EU
Medical Device Regulation (MDR 2017/745) and
the FDA’s BCI regulatory framework — addresses
the safety and efficacy of BCI devices as products,
but not the normative permissibility of their voluntary use by competent adults in non-clinical settings.
It could impose technical standards (reversibility,
cybersecurity, biocompatibility) and require clinical
testing, but it has no jurisdiction over the social and
philosophical questions raised by the Fraternity scenario. Similarly, the Latin American Parlatino Model
Law on Neurorights [17] — the most comprehensive
regional soft-law instrument to date — explicitly conditions its protections on the absence of free, prior,
and informed consent: where consent is present, the
Model Law’s prohibitions do not apply.
General contract law, in most jurisdictions, would
render permanently irrevocable neural access agreements at least partially unenforceable on grounds of
public policy. A contract purporting to grant another
party permanent, irrevocable access to one’s mental states would likely be treated as void ab initio in
most civil law systems on grounds of violation of
human dignity (Germany’s Menschenwürde doctrine,
France’s orders publics restrictions on personality rights contracts) and in common law systems on
grounds of unconscionability or as contrary to public policy. However, the invalidation of contracts
provides no positive normative framework: it tells us
what individuals cannot bind themselves to but provides no guidance about the positive content of the
obligations they bear toward themselves and toward
society in this domain. It is precisely this positive
normative content that the neuroduties framework is
designed to supply.
The conclusion from this survey is that existing
legal frameworks address the Fraternity scenario
partially and inadequately — they can prevent some
of its worst contractual manifestations, but they cannot articulate a principled normative basis for the
constraints they impose, and they cannot address
the social and philosophical harms that the scenario
generates even in its legally unproblematic forms. A
more fundamental normative framework is required.
The Structural Limit: The Problem of Voluntary
Waiver and the Foundations of Neuroduties
The deeper structural limitation of the neurorights
framework is conceptual rather than merely legislative. As a right, mental privacy is, in principle, waivable by its holder. The framework protects against nonconsensual interference but falls silent when consent
is freely given. This is not merely a gap in existing
formulations that could be closed by refinement; it is
a consequence of the conceptual architecture of rights
as such. Rights are, paradigmatically, the property of
the individual subject who holds them, and the paradigmatic way of extinguishing a right’s obligation is
through the rightholder’s informed consent. If I genuinely and freely choose to expose my mind to another,
what principle drawn from within the neurorights
framework could object?
The digital data protection analogy is instructive
and sobering. The GDPR, the California Consumer
Privacy Act, and their equivalents represent some
of the most sophisticated normative architectures in
the history of privacy law. Yet in practice they have
failed to prevent the mass extraction and commercial exploitation of personal data, precisely because
consent can be obtained through adhesion contracts,
interface design, and structural asymmetries in bargaining power [21, 22]. Neural data may become

especially vulnerable to this dynamic as inferential capabilities improve. Its sensitivity is contextual
rather than categorically superior to that of all other
data sources: digital-behavioural data may in some
cases reveal more about an individual than current
neural signals. The distinctive concern is that neural
data can increasingly support inferences about mental processes while carrying a culturally powerful
presumption of direct access to the mind. The neurorights framework as currently formulated provides no
answer to the person who says: "I know my mental
privacy is a right; I waive it freely."
It is for these reasons that we advance the concept
of neuroduties as a complementary normative framework. The concept requires explicit philosophical
grounding — grounding that goes beyond the reference to prior work and engages directly with the liberal autonomy tradition that will constitute its most
formidable critical interlocutor.
The dominant tradition in liberal political philosophy, from Mill’s harm principle to Feinberg’s elaboration of soft paternalism, holds that the state (and by
extension, law) may only restrict individual choices
to prevent harm to others, not to protect individuals
from the consequences of their own free decisions
[23]. On this view, if the Fraternity members harm
only themselves by dissolving their mental privacy,
there is no liberal justification for legal intervention.
Mill’s position in On Liberty is canonical: "Over himself, over his own body and mind, the individual is
sovereign."
We accept the force of this tradition and do not
advocate for paternalism in its objectionable form —
the overriding of competent, informed adult choices
simply because others disagree with them. Our argument is different, and operates on three levels that
are consistent with a sophisticated reading of liberal
autonomy theory. First, the harm-to-others condition is not absent from the Fraternity scenario: the
dissolution of individual selfhood and legal personhood generates harms to third parties who depend
on individuated, responsible agents as counterparties
to contracts, as defendants in legal proceedings, as
parents of children, and as members of social institutions. These are harms to others in the relevant
liberal sense, and they ground a liberal-compatible
objection to certain forms of radical neural self-dissolution. Second, Feinberg’s concept of soft paternalism permits intervention when the relevant choice is
not genuinely autonomous — when it is made under
conditions that defeat the very autonomy its exercise presupposes [23]. The argument we develop in

The inalienability doctrine — familiar from the prohibition of permanent self-enslavement, the unenforceability of certain contracts for the permanent

the substrate whose preservation is a precondition for
the meaningful exercise of any further autonomy, and
constrain accordingly. Thus, one can conclude that
neuroduties are not the negation of autonomy; they
are the recognition that autonomy presupposes a sufficiently stable self — embodied, historical, possessed
of memory and mental privacy — and that actions
which destroy the conditions of this stability destroy
the very precondition for the meaningful exercise of
autonomy.

an insufficiency internal to the rights framework to

Situating the Fraternity Scenario within the Literature
on Collective and Hybrid Minds
Before turning to the Fraternity scenario itself, it
is necessary to situate it explicitly within a body of
scholarship this article has so far engaged too little:
the rapidly growing philosophical and neuroethical literature on technologically mediated collective
minds, hybrid minds, and mind merging. This literature has, in recent years, developed conceptual
resources that bear directly on the questions this article raises, and clarifying how the Fraternity scenario
relates to it is necessary to establish that the argument advanced here identifies a genuine limitation of
the neurorights framework rather than redescribing a
problem already mapped elsewhere.
Danaher and Nyholm [24] ask directly whether
technology should be used to merge minds, and distinguish several routes by which merger might occur
— from loose, information-sharing links to deep,
experience-sharing fusions — while identifying the
loss of individual identity as the central point of ethical contention. Danaher and Petersen [25] go further
and defend, at least provisionally, the permissibility of a "hivemind society" against the objection that
it is necessarily a diminishment of value, arguing
that many of the goods we associate with individual
achievement and experience could in principle be preserved, or even enhanced, in a suitably designed collective mind. Lyreskog, Zohny, Savulescu, and Singh
[26] propose a taxonomy of what they term Technologies for Collective Minds, distinguishing DigiMinds,
UniMinds, NetMinds, and MacroMinds along dimensions of directness and directionality of connection,
and argue that existing frameworks for collective
agency and responsibility are not well equipped to
track the ethically relevant differences between these
configurations. Zohny and Savulescu [27], writing
from the standpoint of clinical neuroethics, analyse
a comparable spectrum running from loosely connected pairs to fully merged minds and ask whether
the individual-focused apparatus of clinical ethics
— informed consent, patient benefit, advance directives — can accommodate mind mergers of varying
degrees, concluding that it can for most configurations short of full fusion but that fully merged cases

## A Descriptive Vignette

Consider the following scenario, constructed to maximise the autonomy-based argument for permissibility. Seven individuals — philosophers, technologists,
and artists, all in their thirties and professionally
accomplished — share a philosophical conviction
that individual selfhood is the primary source of
human suffering: that loneliness, misunderstanding, self-deception, and the opacity of others to one
another are evils that neurotechnology can and should
overcome. They subscribe collectively to an ideology
of absolute transparency and unity of mind, drawing
on traditions ranging from certain strands of Buddhist
non-self philosophy to transhumanist visions of postindividual consciousness. After two years of deliberation, independent legal advice, and psychological evaluation, each voluntarily undergoes bilateral
implantation of high-bandwidth, bidirectional BCIs
specifically configured to establish continuous, realtime neural connectivity with the other six members.
Their stated and documented goal is to constitute a
single mind distributed across seven bodies.
Within the first weeks, members report an experience of unprecedented intimacy and understanding: thoughts arise that no single member can trace
to themselves; decisions emerge from the collective field without any member being able to identify
themselves as their author; emotional states propagate across the network within milliseconds, so that
one member’s grief becomes the grief of all, and one
member’s exhilaration becomes the exhilaration of
all. Members report finding it increasingly difficult to
recall which memories are originally theirs. After six
months, two members wish to disconnect but find —
as the implants are surgically integrated and removal
requires the cooperation of the other five — that exit
is in practice unavailable. The group has incorporated
as a legal entity for contractual purposes, but courts
may be unable to determine whether, and to what
extent, responsibility can be attributed to any individual member for decisions made in the collective
neural field. A precise specification of the exit conditions is required here, since an underspecified account
of how disconnection is registered risks internal
inconsistency. We stipulate: no member can give
first-person testimony about a purely private mental
state, because ex hypothesi no such walled-off state
persists under continuous, total-scope sharing. What
each member retains is a perspectival locus within the
shared stream, and it is at this level — not at the level
of a private, unshared state — that dissent becomes
legible. When we say that "two members wish to
disconnect," this is shorthand for a detectable event:
a sustained divergence originating from a particular
locus that manifests as resistance to, or withdrawal
from, the shared content in a way the other members can register as belonging to that locus specifically. This precisification is doing real argumentative
work in Sects. "First Cluster: Mental Health and the

## Three Clusters of Problems

The Fraternity scenario generates at least three distinct clusters of problems that the neurorights framework, interpreted as a system of individual rights, cannot resolve. The first cluster concerns mental health:
would a fully interconnected mind remain a functional,
healthy mind, or would the dissolution of mental privacy be constitutively pathogenic? The second cluster
concerns identity and responsibility: if the Fraternity
constitutes a single distributed mind, what becomes of
individual selfhood, and with it, of individual responsibility — both moral and legal? The third cluster
concerns the irreducible social and legal functions of
privacy: is mental privacy a preference that some individuals may choose to forgo, or is it a structural precondition of the forms of social and legal life that make
individual autonomy itself possible?
These three clusters are analytically distinct and,
crucially, not of equal normative depth. The first cluster (mental health) is empirically contingent: its force
depends on neuroscientific facts about ego-boundary
maintenance that could in principle be revised by future
evidence. The second cluster (identity and responsibility) is conceptually necessary: it follows from the logic
of personal identity and legal attribution regardless of
empirical detail, but remains open to the possibility
that legal systems could, in principle, be redesigned to
accommodate collective neural entities. The third cluster (structural privacy) is normatively foundational: it
concerns the conditions of possibility of autonomous
agency and legal order themselves — conditions that
cannot be dissolved by technological progress without
eliminating the very concept of the right-holding subject. The argument of this article treats Cluster 3 as the
load-bearing ground for the neuroduties conclusion;
Clusters 1 and 2 provide independent corroborating
arguments of diminishing abstraction. The analysis that
follows is structured to make this hierarchy explicit.
First Cluster: Mental Health and the Boundaries
of Self
A rich tradition in clinical psychology and psychiatry
has established that the capacity to maintain a distinct
inner life — a "private self" not fully accessible to
others — is not merely a cultural preference but a
functional prerequisite for psychological health. The
ability to have thoughts that are not acted upon, emotions that are not expressed, and desires that are not
shared is integral to the regulatory processes by which
the psyche maintains coherence and stability over
time. The experience of one’s thoughts being fully
transparent to others is not, in this tradition, a utopian
ideal of total intimacy; it is a recognised symptom of
severe psychopathology. "Thought insertion" — the
experience of having thoughts placed in one’s mind
by an external agent — and "thought broadcasting"
— the experience of having one’s thoughts transmitted to others without volition — are among the firstrank symptoms of schizophrenia and are consistently
associated with severe distress, disorganisation, and
the breakdown of a coherent sense of self [32].
One might object, consistently with liberal autonomy theory, that the pathological character of these
experiences lies not in thought-exposure as such
but in the absence of voluntary control. The schizophrenic patient cannot activate or deactivate the experience; the Fraternity members, by contrast, choose
their condition and could, in principle, choose to disconnect. This objection has genuine force and must
be engaged directly. Voluntary and involuntary exposure to neural transparency are not equivalent, and a
normative framework that treated them identically
would be over-inclusive. However, the voluntariness objection does not fully resolve the concern for
at least three reasons that take us into the territory of
Feinberg’s soft paternalism [33, 34].
First, the neuroscientific evidence suggests that
the neural substrate of selfhood is constitutively
boundary-dependent in a way that persists regardless
of voluntariness. The default mode network — the
distributed set of brain regions most closely associated with self-referential thought, autobiographical
memory, the sense of agency, and the experience of
a unified perspective across time — is activated characteristically in conditions of internal reflection and
self-directed cognition [35]. Its distinctive functional
signature depends on the existence of a cognitive
boundary between self and other: between the stream
of one’s own thoughts and the streams of others’. The
continuous, real-time influx of another individual’s
mental contents would, on current neuroscientific
understanding, disrupt the very neural processes by

<!-- Page 11 -->

Neuroethics (2026) 19:43 Page 11 of 19 43
which the coherent experience of a single, bounded
perspective is maintained. The question is not
whether this disruption could be desired; the question
is whether the self that emerges from it would retain
the functional properties that make autonomous
choice possible.
Second, the issue of voluntariness under conditions of deep social commitment is substantially more
complex than it appears in the abstract. Members of
close-knit communities — religious orders, romantic
partnerships, political cells — regularly report that
formal exit options, while technically available, are
so costly in practice — given the accumulated social
investments, shared memories, and mutual dependencies already established — as to be effectively unavailable. The Fraternity’s neural bonds would create
a degree of mutual entanglement and co-dependence
that would make disconnection psychologically
equivalent to a kind of annihilation of the shared self.
The freedom to exit, which grounds the voluntariness of entry, may be constitutively undermined by
the very neural integration whose permissibility it is
invoked to justify. The vignette of Sect. "

Third, and most fundamentally, there is the problem of cognitive self-determination under conditions
of permanent neural co-habitation. If the members of

distinction between one’s own thoughts and those
received from others becomes systematically unclear.
The psyche cannot maintain its characteristic regulatory and integrative functions without some minimal

with the most severe and disabling forms of ego fragmentation. This is not paternalism in the objectionable sense of overriding a competent preference: it is
the recognition, familiar from Feinberg’s analysis of
substantially non-autonomous choices, that a choice
whose execution destroys the conditions of its own
voluntariness may not be a fully autonomous choice
at all [23, 34]. A normative framework that permitted the voluntary dissolution of mental privacy at the
neural level would be creating conditions conducive
to widespread psychological harm — harm not fully
articulable in the language of individual rights, but
fully articulable in the language of duties toward oneself and toward society.
Second Cluster: Identity, Individuation,
and Responsibility

## Personal Identity in a Shared Mind

The philosophical literature on personal identity has converged, across a diversity of theoretical approaches, on the view that identity over time
requires some form of psychological continuity: the
persistence of memories, personality traits, values,
projects, and narrative self-understanding that constitute the "same person" across temporal extension
[36]. Whether one adopts a strict psychological continuity theory, a narrative identity theory, or a biological approach, some version of the claim that personal
identity requires a bounded, individuated psychological stream seems difficult to avoid. The question
raised by the Fraternity scenario is whether such psychological continuity is possible when the psychological substrate is continuously co-constituted by the
mental contents of others.
Consider the conditions of the Fraternity in its
fully realised form. Each member continuously shares
in the memories, beliefs, emotional reactions, and
ongoing mental states of six other individuals, and
they in turn share in theirs. Over time, the boundaries between what-I-remember and what-I-haveco-experienced, between what-I-believe and whatI-have-absorbed, between my emotional responses
and those I have received from others, become systematically indeterminate. The narrative self — the
autobiographical story that each person constructs
to make sense of their life as a single, continuous,
first-personal experience — cannot be coherently
maintained under these conditions: there is no longer
a single protagonist, only a collective subject whose
temporal and interpersonal boundaries are fluid [37].
This is not a marginal or speculative consequence of
the scenario; it is its explicit aspiration, stated by the
Fraternity’s own ideology of the dissolution of individual selfhood. Of course, a defender of merging
might argue that psychological continuity need not
disappear but could migrate from the individual to the
collective level: the post-merger subject might remain
psychologically continuous with each pre-merger

participant. We accept that this is a coherent possibility. Our claim is therefore not that all psychological
continuity necessarily vanishes, but that individual
continuity becomes non-unique and potentially nonbranching only at the cost of reconceiving the relevant subject of identity. That reconception is precisely
what destabilises legal attribution to the pre-existing
natural persons.

## The Collapse of Moral and Legal Responsibility

The collapse of individual selfhood has direct and
severe consequences for moral and legal responsibility. Our practices of holding individuals responsible
— praising and blaming, punishing and rewarding,
recognising rights and imposing obligations — all
presuppose that actions can be attributed to specific,
identifiable agents who formed the intentions that
caused them, who had the reasons and the capacity
to have done otherwise, and who are the appropriate
recipients of the reactive attitudes those actions warrant [38]. In a fully interconnected mind, individual
attribution may become radically underdetermined,
and in the maximal case potentially conceptually
indeterminate. If a decision to perform an action
arose in a mental space continuously inhabited by
the thoughts, desires, fears, and beliefs of seven individuals, the concept of individual authorship — and
with it, individual responsibility — dissolves. This
is precisely the point at which the extended-mind
analyses of mental integrity developed by Cassinadri
[28], Cassinadri and Ienca [29], and Tesink et al. [30]
become relevant, though their conclusion runs in the
opposite direction from ours. Those accounts argue
that where a device becomes a constitutive part of an
individual’s cognitive system, the scope of that individual’s rights over the device expands accordingly.
The Fraternity case shows the limit of that extension
strategy: it works only so long as there remains one
individuated subject whose cognitive system is being
extended. Once the sharing is genuinely mutual and
total, there is no longer a single subject-of-extension to whom rights or duties can be anchored, and
the extended-mind framework, designed for one
mind extended through a tool, does not straightforwardly generalise to several minds mutually extended
through one another.
The legal consequences are severe across multiple
domains. Criminal law in all major legal traditions
— common law, civil law, and mixed systems — presupposes individual agency: a defendant must have
formed the relevant mental state (mens rea) and performed the relevant physical act (actus reus) as an
individual whose mental processes can be attributed
to them specifically. Contract law presupposes that
the contracting parties are distinct, identifiable legal
persons whose wills are their own and whose consent
is their own act. Family law, property law, and virtually every domain of private law rest on the assumption that the human subjects they regulate are individuated agents whose decisions can be attributed to
them and whose legal positions are separate.
A comparative survey of how legal systems treat
analogous problems of collective agency reveals both
the resources available and their limitations. Corporate
law, which has the most developed framework for collective decision-making, accommodates legal persons
that are not natural individuals through the mechanism
of the corporate veil: the corporation acts through designated officers, and its acts can be attributed to specific natural persons through the chain of command.
But the corporate analogy fails precisely at the point
where it would be most needed for the Fraternity scenario: corporate acts are attributed to specific directors, managers, or agents whose individual agency
remains intact. It is precisely individual agency that
the Fraternity scenario dissolves. Partnership law,
joint enterprise doctrine in criminal law, and the law
of conspiracy all permit the attribution of acts to collective agents, but each depends on the ability to identify individuals who formed individual intentions and
made individual choices to act together. Continuous
neural co-constitution eliminates this analytic base.
One might respond that the law is adaptable, and
that new legal frameworks could accommodate collective neural entities through radical revision of the
attribution rules. Some scholars have explored the
concept of "group agency" in philosophy and suggested that it might ground a form of collective legal
personhood distinct from both natural persons and
corporations [9]. This is true in principle, but the cost
must be acknowledged: the abandonment of individual legal personhood as the fundamental unit of
a rights-and-duties framework developed over centuries of legal thought across diverse traditions, and
the loss of the principle of personal responsibility that
underlies the entire structure of criminal, contractual,
and tortious liability. More significantly, the redesign

of legal systems to accommodate collective neural
entities would not be a neutral technical adjustment:
it would require the abandonment of the concept of
individual guilt, the concept of individual contractual
will, and the concept of individual rights as subjective
entitlements of a personal rightholder. The question is
not only whether the Fraternity scenario is technically
manageable within reformed legal frameworks; it is
whether its normalisation is compatible with the basic
architecture of law as a system for governing the conduct of individuated, responsible persons.
Responsibility Without Attribution: The Systemic
Dimension
There is a further, systemic consequence of attribution collapse that deserves independent analysis.
Legal systems are not merely sets of rules for resolving individual disputes; they are information-processing systems that depend on the legibility of individual
mental states through evidentiary and inferential procedures. Courts reconstruct mental states — intention, knowledge, belief, recklessness — from behaviour, testimony, and circumstantial evidence, precisely
because they cannot directly access the minds of the
persons before them. This epistemic limitation is not
a deficiency to be remedied but a constitutive feature
of a fair legal process: it is why no one can be compelled to testify against themselves, why confessions
obtained under duress are inadmissible, and why the
standard of proof in criminal proceedings is "beyond
reasonable doubt" rather than "beyond neural doubt."
A society in which some individuals’ mental states
are continuously and directly accessible to others —
including, potentially, to law enforcement — does
not have a more efficient legal system; it has a fundamentally different and far more dangerous one, in
which the formal equality of persons before the law is
destroyed by radical epistemic inequality.
Third Cluster: The Irreducible Functions
of Privacy
Privacy as a Structural Precondition of Autonomous
Agency
The deepest cluster of problems raised by the Fraternity scenario concerns the social functions of privacy
that go beyond its value as a preference of individual
rightsholders. A rich tradition in political philosophy and social theory — from Arendt’s analysis of
the distinction between public and private realms, to
Floridi’s information-philosophical account of "ontological friction," to Goffman’s sociological analysis
of self-presentation and identity management — has
converged on the view that privacy is not simply one
good among others but a structural precondition for
the forms of social, political, and legal life that make
individual autonomy possible [39–41].
Arendt’s argument is particularly illuminating for
the neural case. For Arendt [39], the private realm is
not a refuge from political life but its precondition:
only a subject who has a protected sphere of interiority — who is not wholly visible to others, who retains
a domain of thought and action not yet disclosed —
can appear in the public realm as a genuine agent,
capable of action and speech that are genuinely theirs
rather than performances shaped entirely by the gaze
of others. The destruction of the private sphere does
not liberate the individual into fuller participation in
political and social life; it destroys the subject capable of such participation. Applied to the neural case: a
person whose thoughts are continuously accessible to
others cannot maintain the internal deliberative space
in which genuinely autonomous choices are formed.
The experience of permanent neural observation —
even by trusted friends, even by people one loves —
introduces an inescapable dimension of performance
and self-censorship into the innermost processes of
thought.
Foucault’s [42] analysis of the panopticon demonstrates that the mere awareness of potential surveillance transforms behaviour at the level of self-regulation and self-presentation; the certainty of permanent
neural exposure to known others would eliminate the
space of authentic, unperformed mental action altogether. The self-constituted under conditions of permanent neural transparency would be a deeply different kind of self — not a freer or more authentic one,
but one whose thoughts are permanently shaped by
their audience. From the perspective of information
philosophy, Floridi’s [40] concept of "ontological
friction" captures a related point. The resistance that
separates information environments from one another
— the effort required to transmit information across
boundaries — is not merely an obstacle to communication but a constitutive feature of informational and

Vol:. (1234567890)
personal identity. A self without ontological friction,
whose information is fully and continuously merged
with that of others, is not a richer, better-connected
self; it is a dissolved self, one that can no longer distinguish its own informational territory from the
shared landscape in which it is immersed.
It is at this level — the structural precondition
level — that the liberal autonomy argument is most
fundamentally answered. The liberal tradition values privacy instrumentally, as a condition for the
development and exercise of individual autonomy.
Thinkers in the liberal tradition from Mill to Rawls
have recognised that a sphere of protected individual
space is not a constraint on autonomy but its enabling
condition. The argument from structural privacy is,
therefore, not an argument against liberalism but an
argument from within liberalism’s own deepest commitments: individual autonomy cannot be sustained
without individual mental privacy, and a choice to
permanently dissolve the latter is a choice to permanently disable the former.
The Asymmetric Variant: Neural Dominion
The problems multiply dramatically in the asymmetric variant of the scenario: the case in which one
member of the group — the Overseer — retains the
capacity to read the mental states of the others while
maintaining their own mental privacy. This variant is
not merely a theoretical modification; it corresponds
to a highly realistic scenario in which the founder or
leader of a group conditions membership on the surrender of mental privacy while retaining their own.
The asymmetric case does not require malicious
intent on the part of the Overseer; it can arise naturally from structural inequalities of power within the
group, from claims of spiritual authority, or simply
from the technological configuration of the neural
network.
We propose the term neural dominion for this
configuration, defined by the following necessary
and sufficient conditions: (NC1) agent A has continuous, real-time, high-fidelity read-access to the
propositional mental states of agent B; (NC2) B
cannot acquire equivalent read-access to A’s mental
states; (NC3) the asymmetry specified in NC1–NC2
is structural — built into the architecture of the neural
interface — rather than merely contingent on momentary choice; and (NC4) the configuration is persistent
rather than episodic. Agent A stands in a relation of
neural dominion over B if and only if NC1–NC4 are
jointly satisfied.
In this asymmetric configuration, the Overseer
possesses complete, real-time information about each
member’s thoughts, beliefs, fears, desires, and internal deliberations, while the members cannot access
the Overseer’s mind. The power differential that
results is not merely extreme in degree; it is maximal
in kind. No blackmailer, no interrogator, no surveillance apparatus has ever possessed information about
another person as complete and continuous as the
Overseer possesses in this scenario. The members of
the group are permanently and totally exposed while
the Overseer remains permanently opaque.
The case for treating neural dominion as categorically impermissible — not merely dangerous in
practice but normatively impermissible in principle,
regardless of the formal consent of the dominated
parties — rests on two convergent arguments. The
first is the argument from the self-defeating character of the consent: the right to mental privacy cannot
be validly surrendered in a fully binding, permanent,
asymmetric way because the act of surrender destroys
the very faculty — the autonomous, self-determining
subject — whose consent gave the surrender its purported validity. Consent that cancels its own presuppositions cannot ground genuine autonomy. The second argument is the argument from the preconditions
of legal order: a functioning legal system presupposes
that its subjects are individuated, mutually opaque
agents, and a society in which some individuals have
direct neural access to others’ minds does not have a
more efficient or just legal system but a fundamentally more dangerous one in which formal legal equality is destroyed by epistemic inequality.
Privacy as Both Right and Duty
The foregoing analysis suggests that mental privacy,
in the neural context, has a normative status that cannot be captured by the concept of a subjective right
alone. It possesses at least two further normative
dimensions that the rights framework cannot articulate [43].
The first is the dimension of inalienability: a
protection that cannot be validly waived in certain
forms because its waiver would destroy the legal
and moral subject whose protection the right is

designed to guarantee. The classical analogy is with
the right not to be enslaved: a contract of permanent
self-enslavement is void in most legal systems not
because freedom is unimportant but because its permanent, unconditional surrender contradicts the very
autonomy invoked to validate the surrender [23]. The
German Basic Law’s protection of Menschenwürde
— human dignity — as the one constitutional guarantee that admits of no limitation, not even by the
rights-holder’s own consent, provides a constitutional
expression of this inalienability logic. The French
Conseil Constitutionnel’s doctrine that the "essential
content" of fundamental rights cannot be legislated
away even by parliamentary supermajority provides
another. The structural argument applies with equal
force to the permanent surrender of mental privacy:
a person cannot validly consent to becoming the permanently readable subject of another’s neural dominion, because the conditions of valid consent — deliberative autonomy, the capacity for reflective choice,
access to unobserved mental space — are precisely
what the surrender destroys.
The second is the dimension of relational duty:
an obligation that flows not only from the interest of
the individual rightholder but from the interest of all
those — partners, dependants, co-citizens, legal counterparties — who depend on that individual maintaining the conditions of stable, responsible personhood.
The duty to preserve sufficient mental privacy is
analogous to duties of physical self-preservation that
most legal systems recognise in specific contexts (the
duty not to assume obligations one cannot perform,
the duty to maintain legal capacity, the duty not to
create conditions of permanent incapacity in oneself):
it protects not only the individual but the social and
legal practices — responsibility, trust, authentic communication, contractual reliability — that presuppose
the individual’s continued existence as a bounded,
individuated agent.
This is the core insight of the neuroduties framework as applied to the Fraternity scenario. The claim
that certain forms of consensual neural interconnection should be constrained is not paternalism in the
objectionable sense — a refusal to respect the preferences of competent adults on the ground that others know better what is good for them. It is the
recognition, consistent with Feinberg’s soft paternalism and with the liberal tradition’s own internal
logic, that some choices are self-undermining in a
philosophically precise sense: they destroy the conditions — bounded selfhood, psychological coherence,
individual responsibility, the capacity for genuine
autonomous choice — on which the very concept of
meaningful consent depends. The claim is therefore
transcendental rather than paternalistic: autonomy
cannot justify eliminating the conditions that make
autonomous agency conceptually possible in the
first place. A self that has dissolved into a collective
neural network is not a freer self that has exercised
its autonomy to the fullest extent; it is a self that has
consumed the very autonomy it was exercising in the
act of exercise.
Conclusions: Neurorights, Neuroduties,
and the Irreducibility of Privacy
This article has argued that the neurorights framework, while representing a genuine and important
advance in the ethical and legal governance of neurotechnology, contains a structural limitation that
refinement internal to its individualist architecture is,
on the argument developed here, unlikely to remedy:
it was designed to protect individuals from non-consensual third-party interference but appears unable
to fully address scenarios in which individuals voluntarily choose to waive or dissolve their own mental privacy through neural interconnection. The right
to mental privacy is, as a subjective right, waivable
— and the dominant consent-centred formulations of
the neurorights framework do not yet provide a sufficiently developed account of why certain waivers
should be treated as invalid.
We have shown that existing legal frameworks
— data protection law, medical device regulation,
and general contract law — can address some of the
Fraternity scenario’s worst manifestations, but cannot provide a principled normative basis for the constraints they impose, and cannot address the social
and philosophical harms the scenario generates even
in its legally tolerated forms. The GDPR may not
reach genuinely decentralised neural exchanges confined to natural persons acting within a purely personal sphere, and even where it applies it does not
resolve the permissibility of the underlying self-transformative practice; medical device regulation governs
the safety of products, not the permissibility of their
use; and the invalidation of irrevocable neural access

agreements under contract law tells us what individuals cannot bind themselves to but provides no positive

The Fraternity scenario — the voluntary formation

that purportedly authorises neural dominion destroys
its own preconditions in the act of being given, and
the legal order that permitted neural dominion would
sacrifice the formal equality of persons before the law
on the altar of epistemic inequality.
These considerations support the theoretical
necessity of a neuroduties framework as a complement — not a replacement — to the neurorights
paradigm. Neurorights address the non-consensual
dimension of neurotechnological threat; neuroduties address the consensual dimension. The philosophical foundation of neuroduties, as developed
in this article, rests on three converging grounds:
the empirical claims about the neuropsychological
conditions of selfhood (Cluster 1); the conceptual
claims about the logical presuppositions of personal
identity and legal attribution (Cluster 2); and the
normative claims about the structural conditions
of autonomous agency and legal order (Cluster 3).
Together they constitute a more complete account
of what the emergence of advanced neurotechnology demands of ethics and law — one that engages
honestly with the liberal autonomy tradition rather
than dismissing it, and that extends the inalienability logic already present in liberal legal systems to
the neural domain.
Important questions remain open. The precise
thresholds at which neural interconnection crosses
from the permissible to the legally and morally
impermissible must be theorised with care, distinguishing the full spectrum of scenarios from minimal BBI to continuous total neural transparency.
The legal instruments through which neuroduties
might be operationalised — capacity assessments,
limits on the enforceability of neural access agreements, mandatory reversibility requirements, temporal limits on neural commitments — require
detailed elaboration across different legal traditions,
from German constitutional dignity law to AngloAmerican consent doctrine to the emerging Latin
American neurorights framework. And the question
of international governance demands urgent attention: absent coordinated global standards, the risks
of unregulated neural communes and of "neurotelepathy tourism" beyond the reach of any national
legal order are real and growing [44].
What this article has sought to establish is the
principled foundation for a conclusion: that certain aspects of human mental life are not goods that
their possessors may trade away unconditionally,
but structural features of the human person whose
preservation is a condition not only of individual
flourishing but of the continued possibility of the
social, moral, and legal forms of life that make
human communities — and human freedom — possible. Privacy, at its deepest, is not merely a right to
be exercised or waived: on the argument developed
here, it also functions as a duty. The future governance of neurotechnology therefore requires not only
a catalogue of rights protecting the mind from others, but also a theory of duties protecting the conditions under which minds can remain the kinds of
entities to which rights meaningfully attach [45].

Note 1. The range of the scenario is deliberately specified. At
the minimal end (binary BBI for cooperative task performance),
ethical concerns are modest and may be governed by existing
frameworks for medical devices and informed consent. It is the
richer end — continuous, high-fidelity, bidirectional sharing of
propositional thought, emotion, and autobiographical memory
— that generates the deep problems analysed in this article.
Note 2. The analogy with slavery is philosophically precise,
not merely rhetorical. Most legal systems treat certain personal
attributes — bodily liberty, the capacity for legal personhood
itself — as inalienable: incapable of permanent transfer even
by the voluntary act of their holder. A contract of permanent
self-enslavement is void not because the relevant preference
is insufficiently intense but because the autonomy required
to validate the contract cannot survive its execution [23].
The present argument applies the same structural logic to the
epistemic dimension: a permanent transfer of the capacity for
mental privacy to an Overseer is void, because the autonomous
subject whose consent was required for the transfer cannot
persist in conditions of permanent neural dominion. Note 3. The
international governance dimension deserves separate, extended
treatment beyond what is possible here. Absent coordinated
global standards, jurisdictions with weaker protections risk
becoming destinations for groups seeking to establish neural
communes prohibited elsewhere — a form of"neurotelepathy
tourism" structurally analogous to reproductive tourism or
assisted dying tourism. The UNESCO (2025) recommendation
and the UN Human Rights Council (2024) report on
neurotechnology are first steps toward the multilateral
framework that would be required. For a detailed analysis of the
governance ecosystem challenges, see Winickoff and Kreiling
(2024). Note 4. Four instruments merit brief elaboration.
(1) Capacity assessments: independent neurological and
psychological evaluation of whether a prospective participant
genuinely understands the identity, responsibility, and privacy
implications of permanent neural interconnection — analogous
to competency assessments in medical consent law but extended
to cover the specific cognitive distortions that social pressure and
ideological commitment to neural transparency can produce.
(2) Limits on the enforceability of neural access agreements:
treating contracts that purport to grant permanent, irrevocable
neural access as void ab initio on grounds of inalienability, on
